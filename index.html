A self-set challenge · 2026

Seven days from first principles to Einstein's field equations.

Starting from Maxwell's equations, I gave myself one week to reach general relativity — and to be able to rebuild it from a blank page, not just follow it. This is the path I took and the notes I drew for the climb.

by Muhammad Anas  ·  Sargodha, Pakistan
the climb
Why

Following a derivation and reproducing it are different skills. Only the second one survives the week.

So I set a rule. Each day ended with a milestone that was a task I could either do or not do — a derivation reproduced from memory, a number computed from scratch — never a topic I had merely read. The two hardest gates were Day 3, where the relativity of simultaneity has to stop feeling like a trick, and Day 6, tensor calculus, where the whole plan nearly broke.

The route

Seven milestones.

Day 1–2

Maxwell, each equation earned

Gauss from Coulomb, Faraday from experiment, the displacement current forced by charge conservation. Curl of curl — and light falls out at \(1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\).

Day 3

Special relativity

The contradiction between a fixed \(c\) and Galilean addition. Two postulates, the Lorentz transformation from scratch, and \(E=mc^2\). The deceptive day.

Day 4

Minkowski spacetime

The interval \(ds^2=-c^2dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2\) becomes an object, not a formula. Light cones, proper time, and the equivalence principle that breaks SR.

Day 5

Curvature, intrinsically

Gauss's Theorema Egregium: curvature measurable from inside, no embedding needed. Then Riemann frees it from surfaces into \(n\) dimensions.

Day 6

Tensor calculus — the hard day

Why \(\partial_i V^k\) isn't a tensor. Christoffel symbols from metric-compatibility, the covariant derivative, geodesics, and Riemann as the commutator \([\nabla_i,\nabla_j]\).

Day 7

The field equations

Why \(G_{\mu\nu}\) and nothing else. The Newtonian limit fixing \(8\pi G/c^4\). Schwarzschild, and Mercury's 43″.

The proof it stuck

Two arguments I can now make from a blank page.

A challenge like this is only worth anything if something survives it. These are the two results that, to me, are general relativity in miniature: first the argument that the left-hand side of the field equations is forced — it could be nothing else — and then the payoff, Mercury's orbit matching reality to the arcsecond, with no free parameters.

Derivation I

Why the left side must be \(G_{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12 R\,g_{\mu\nu}\)

The claim of general relativity is that matter curves spacetime. So the field equation should read: some measure of curvature, on the left, equals the source of gravity, on the right. The right side is forced first — the source of gravity is energy and momentum, described by the stress–energy tensor \(T_{\mu\nu}\). That fixes what the left side has to be.

Constraint 1 — it must be a rank-2 symmetric tensor

\(T_{\mu\nu}\) is a symmetric rank-2 tensor, so whatever sits opposite it must match: symmetric, two indices, built from the geometry.

Constraint 2 — built from the metric, at most second derivatives

The gravitational field is the metric \(g_{\mu\nu}\). To recover Newton — where the field obeys \(\nabla^2\phi=4\pi G\rho\), a second-order equation — the left side may contain \(g_{\mu\nu}\) and its first and second derivatives, but no higher. The only curvature object meeting this is built from the Riemann tensor, contracted down to the Ricci tensor \(R_{\mu\nu}\) and scalar \(R\).

Constraint 3 — it must be divergence-free

Energy and momentum are conserved locally: \(\nabla^\mu T_{\mu\nu}=0\). If the equation is to hold, the left side must be divergence-free automatically, as an identity of geometry — otherwise the field equation would silently impose an extra constraint on matter it has no right to impose.

The Bianchi identity supplies exactly one candidate

The contracted Bianchi identities — a pure fact about Riemannian geometry — say precisely that

\[\nabla^\mu\!\left(R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12 R\,g_{\mu\nu}\right)=0.\]

That combination, and no other built from the same ingredients, is identically conserved. Call it \(G_{\mu\nu}\). Every constraint is now satisfied at once, and the only remaining freedom is an additive term \(\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}\) (itself divergence-free) — the cosmological constant. Matching the weak-field limit to Newton fixes the proportionality, and the equation is forced:

\[G_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}.\]

Nothing here was chosen for elegance. Each piece is the only thing that fits the one before it. That is what I mean by an equation you can defend.

Derivation II

Mercury's 43″ per century, from scratch

The field equations are only worth trusting if they touch something real. Mercury is that thing. Its orbit's perihelion advances by an amount Newtonian gravity cannot fully explain — a residual of about 43 arcseconds per century that had sat unexplained since Le Verrier in 1859.

The orbit equation gains one term

For the Schwarzschild geometry around the Sun, the Newtonian orbit equation in the variable \(u=1/r\) picks up a single extra piece on the right:

\[\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}+u=\frac{GM}{h^2}+\frac{3GM}{c^2}u^2.\]

The first term is pure Newton — it gives a closed ellipse. The second term, smaller by a factor of order \((v/c)^2\sim10^{-7}\), is the relativistic correction. Tiny, but it never averages away.

A tiny term, but it accumulates

Treating that term as a perturbation, the ellipse no longer closes: its axis rotates a little each orbit. Working it through, the perihelion advances per orbit by

\[\Delta\varphi=\frac{6\pi GM}{c^2 a(1-e^2)}.\]

Putting in Mercury's numbers

With \(GM_\odot=1.327\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}\), semi-major axis \(a=5.79\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m}\), and eccentricity \(e=0.206\):

\[\Delta\varphi\approx5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{rad/orbit}.\]

Mercury completes about 415 orbits per century, so

\[\Delta\varphi_{\text{century}}\approx2.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{rad}\approx 43''.\]

The orphaned residual, recovered exactly — with no parameter free to adjust. This is the moment the whole week paid off: the geometry on the left of Derivation I, carried all the way down to a number a telescope measured a century and a half ago.
