Part I

Greek Mathematics: Proof, Magnitude, and the Conics

The Greeks added — and it is the addition — proof: the demand that mathematical truths be demonstrated from accepted principles.

Ch. 1

~570–495 BCE
Pythagoras

Pythagoras: the Theorem, the Proof, and the Crisis of the Irrational

The Babylonians knew "Pythagorean" number facts a millennium before Pythagoras. What the Greeks added — and it is the addition — is proof: the demand that mathematical truths be demonstrated from accepted principles, not verified case by case.

The theorem \(a^2+b^2=c^2\) (rearrangement proof). Take a square of side \(a+b\). Place four copies of the right triangle in its corners, hypotenuses inward: they enclose a tilted square of side \(c\), so \[(a+b)^2 = 4\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}ab + c^2 \implies a^2+2ab+b^2 = 2ab+c^2 \implies a^2+b^2=c^2.\] Rearranging the same four triangles to leave two squares of sides \(a\) and \(b\) gives a purely visual proof with no algebra at all.

Why physics cares: this theorem is the Euclidean metric. "Distance" in flat space, \(ds^2 = dx^2+dy^2+dz^2\), is Pythagoras infinitesimally; special relativity modifies the signs (\(ds^2 = -c^2dt^2+dx^2+\cdots\)), and general relativity makes the coefficients variable (\(ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu\)). The entire metric tradition of Part V is the biography of this one equation.

Irrationality of \(\sqrt{2}\). Suppose \(\sqrt{2}=p/q\) in lowest terms. Then \(p^2=2q^2\), so \(p\) is even: \(p=2r\). Then \(q^2=2r^2\), so \(q\) is even too — contradicting "lowest terms." This is the model proof by contradiction, the logical weapon Greek mathematics bequeathed to all later science.

Ch. 2–3

~300 BCE
Euclid · Archimedes

Euclid and Archimedes: Axioms and Proto-Integration

Euclid's Elements (~300 BCE) derived ~465 propositions from 5 postulates — the template of exact knowledge that Newton used for the Principia and Einstein absorbed as a child. Postulate 5 (the parallel postulate) is the fuse of Part V: deny it and you get new, consistent geometries — the mathematical precondition of general relativity.

Eudoxus's method of exhaustion rigorously proved statements about curved areas by trapping regions between inscribed and circumscribed polygons — the \(\varepsilon\)-argument, twenty-two centuries before Weierstrass.

Archimedes' circle (\(A=\pi r^2\)). Slice the disk into \(n\) thin pie-wedges and re-stand them alternately up/down: they nearly form a rectangle of height \(r\) and base \(C/2\). Exhaustion turns "nearly" into a proof, giving \(A = \tfrac{1}{2}rC = \pi r^2\). Bounding \(\pi\) by inscribed and circumscribed 96-gons: \[3\tfrac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\tfrac{1}{7},\quad\text{i.e. }3.1408 < \pi < 3.1429\] — the first error-controlled computation in history.

Quadrature of the parabola. The area between a parabola and a chord is \(\tfrac{4}{3}\) the area of the inscribed triangle of the same base and height. Fill with triangles: each stage adds \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) of the previous. Summing: \(T\!\left(1+\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{16}+\cdots\right) = \tfrac{4}{3}T\). In modern terms he evaluated \(\int x^2\,dx\) — 1,900 years before Newton.

Apollonius's Conics (~200 BCE) named the ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola and proved their focus-reflection properties. For 1,800 years it was a museum piece — until Kepler found planets on ellipses, Galileo found projectiles on parabolas, and Newton proved inverse-square orbits are exactly Apollonius's conics. No better advertisement exists for mathematics stockpiled ahead of need.

Part II

Algebra and the Analytic Turn

A curve is an equation; an equation is a curve. Space becomes addressable by numbers.

Ch. 5–6

~820–1637
Algebra · analytic geometry

Algebra, Logarithms, and Analytic Geometry

Al-Khwārizmī (~820 CE) wrote the first systematic treatise on solving equations, classifying quadratics and solving each type by completing the square. The cubic (Cardano, 1545) forced the acceptance of complex numbers: the formula for three real roots demands \(\sqrt{-1}\) en route, and Bombelli showed that calculating boldly makes the imaginary parts cancel. Complex numbers entered mathematics not by choice but by necessity.

Completing the square = the quadratic formula. For \(x^2+bx=c\), add the corner \((b/2)^2\): \[(x+\tfrac{b}{2})^2 = c+\tfrac{b^2}{4} \implies x = -\tfrac{b}{2}+\sqrt{c+\tfrac{b^2}{4}},\] which for the general \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) is \(x = \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\).

Descartes and Fermat (1637): a curve is an equation; an equation is a curve. Coordinates translate geometric problems into algebra and back. Apollonius's conics become simple quadratic equations; space itself becomes addressable by numbers — the precondition for "the state of a system as a point in phase space" (Hamilton) and for coordinates on curved manifolds (Riemann), where GR insists the physics must not depend on the addressing scheme.

Logarithms (Napier 1614): \(\log(xy) = \log x + \log y\) — multiplication collapses into addition. Kepler was among the first heavy users. The hidden treasure: the natural logarithm's link to \(e\), and to the area under \(y=1/x\).

Part III

The Calculus and Analysis

One glance at a sliver, and Archimedes' case-by-case exhaustions become a mechanical calculus.

Ch. 8

1665–1686
Newton · Leibniz

Newton and Leibniz: The Calculus

Newton (1665–66, plague years): variables are "fluents" flowing in time; their rates are fluxions. Master tool: infinite series — the binomial theorem extended to arbitrary exponents, turning every curve into a power series that could be differentiated and integrated term by term.

Leibniz (published 1684/86): differentials \(dx\), \(dy\); the integral sign \(\int\); \(dy/dx\) behaving like a genuine ratio, making the chain rule almost automatic. His notation won — it powers the analytical mechanics of Lagrange.

The derivative, 17th-century style. For \(y=x^2\): \(y+dy = (x+dx)^2 = x^2+2x\,dx+(dx)^2\), giving \(dy=2x\,dx\) after discarding the "infinitely smaller" \((dx)^2\). In general: \(\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}\) (binomial theorem, keep the linear term).

The Fundamental Theorem. Let \(A(x)\) be the area under \(y=f(t)\) from \(a\) to \(x\). Increase \(x\) by a sliver \(dx\): the added area is a near-rectangle of height \(f(x)\) and width \(dx\), so \[\frac{dA}{dx} = f(x) \implies \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)\] where \(F'=f\). One glance at a sliver, and Archimedes' case-by-case exhaustions become a mechanical calculus. All of mathematical physics — "the distance is the integral of the velocity," "the field is the derivative of the potential" — is applications of this theorem.

Ch. 9

1707–1783
Euler

The 18th Century: Euler, Series, Differential Equations

Euler (1707–1783) organized calculus into the shape we still teach and made the function the central object of mathematics. Notation we owe him: \(f(x)\), \(e\), \(i\), \(\pi\) (popularized), \(\Sigma\), sin, cos as functions rather than table entries.

Taylor series. Assume \(f(x) = a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+\cdots\) near 0 and differentiate repeatedly: \[f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\,x^k.\] Physics' universal approximation device: every smooth law is linear at first order and quadratic at second — which is why small oscillations are harmonic, why \(\gamma \approx 1+\tfrac{1}{2}v^2/c^2\) gives Newton back from Einstein, and why "weak-field limits" exist at all.

Euler's formula. Apply Taylor to \(e^{i\theta}\) and sort real from imaginary parts: \[e^{i\theta} = \underbrace{1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}-\cdots}_{\cos\theta} + i\,\underbrace{\left(\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\cdots\right)}_{\sin\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta.\] Rotation, oscillation, and exponential growth are one phenomenon. Waves become \(e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\); interference becomes addition of complex phases; linear ODEs reduce to algebra on exponents.

Ch. 10

1696–1788
Calculus of variations

The Calculus of Variations

The brachistochrone (1696): down which curve does a bead slide between two points in least time? Answer: the cycloid — the same curve Huygens found isochronous. A mechanics problem solved by optics: the first hint that minimum principles unify physics.

Euler and Lagrange: to make \(J[y] = \int F(x,y,y')\,dx\) stationary, \(y\) must satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equation \(\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=0\). When physicists say a theory is "defined by its Lagrangian," they are speaking 18th-century mathematics.

Geodesics as a variational problem — the bridge to tensors. On any space with line element \(ds\), the shortest path makes \(\int ds\) stationary. Applying Euler–Lagrange to \(F = \sqrt{g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j}\) and choosing arc length as parameter gives: \[\ddot x^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j = 0,\] with the \(\Gamma\)'s built from derivatives of \(g_{ij}\). In flat space with Cartesian coordinates all \(\Gamma=0\): straight lines. The same equation with the metric of spacetime is free fall in general relativity — "particles follow geodesics" is the brachistochrone tradition arriving at its destination.

Hilbert derives the Einstein field equations in November 1915 by varying the action \(\int R\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x\). Noether's 1918 theorem (born from GR's energy puzzles) shows every continuous symmetry of an action yields a conservation law.

Ch. 11

1807–1872
Fourier · Cauchy · Weierstrass

Rigor: Cauchy, Fourier, and the Arithmetization of Analysis

Fourier (1807/1822) claimed any function — even one drawn freehand with corners and jumps — equals a trigonometric series. The coefficients follow from orthogonality:

Fourier coefficients. Seek \(f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}(a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx)\). Multiply by \(\cos nx\) and integrate: every term but one dies (orthogonality), giving \[a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)\cos nx\,dx.\] "Projecting a function onto basis vectors": the germ of infinite-dimensional function spaces. Physics payoff: heat conduction and wave problems split into independently evolving modes; the blackbody problem that ends the classical era is precisely mode-counting à la Fourier.

Cauchy (1821) redefined the basics via limits: the derivative as \(\lim_{h\to 0}[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h\); the definite integral as a limit of sums. Weierstrass (1850s–70s) made the limit precise with the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition. Dedekind and Cantor (1872) finally constructed the real numbers, closing the circle opened by Hippasus's \(\sqrt{2}\) — magnitudes and numbers reunited after 2,300 years.

Part IV

The Mathematics of Fields and Frames

Physics after 1905 is invariant theory with experimental consequences.

Ch. 12–13

1820s–1872
Vectors · matrices

Vector Analysis and Linear Algebra

Gibbs and Heaviside (1880s–90s) stripped Hamilton's quaternions to the working essentials — the vector \(\mathbf{A}\) with dot and cross products — creating the notation in which Maxwell's equations are four, not twenty.

Divergence and the divergence theorem. Take an infinitesimal box, sides \(dx,dy,dz\). Net outflow through the two \(x\)-faces: \(\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}dV\). Sum the three directions: outflow \(= (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F})\,dV\). Tile any region: internal faces cancel in pairs, leaving only the boundary: \[\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int_V \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV.\] This is the dictionary between the integral laws experimentalists measure (flux through a surface) and the differential laws theorists solve (Maxwell's equations): the two forms are related by exactly this theorem.

Cayley (1858) defined matrix algebra. The eigenvalue problem — find \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\) — arose inside physics for principal axes of rotating rigid bodies. Sylvester's law of inertia (1852): the counts of \(+\) and \(-\) diagonal entries are invariant — the signature. This will label the difference between Euclidean space \((+++)\) and Minkowski spacetime \((-+++)\).

Klein's Erlangen Program (1872): a geometry is the study of invariants of a transformation group. Special relativity is the geometry of the Lorentz group; general covariance extends the group to all smooth coordinate changes. Physics after 1905 is invariant theory with experimental consequences.

Part V

Geometry Beyond Euclid — the Road to 1915

"Curved space" needs no higher-dimensional space to curve into. Whatever geometry spacetime has can be defined and measured from inside.

Ch. 14

1733–1868
Non-Euclidean geometry

The Parallel Postulate and Non-Euclidean Geometry

Two thousand years of attempts to prove Postulate 5 from the rest end by discovering it is optional. Gauss (privately, from ~1810s), Bolyai ("out of nothing I have created a strange new universe," 1832), and Lobachevsky (1829) independently developed hyperbolic geometry: through a point off a line pass infinitely many non-meeting lines; triangle angles sum to less than 180°, the deficit proportional to area.

Angle excess on the sphere. Take the triangle with a vertex at the north pole and two vertices on the equator, a quarter-circumference apart: all three angles are right angles — sum 270°, excess 90°. Its area is \(\tfrac{1}{8}\cdot 4\pi R^2 = \tfrac{\pi R^2}{2}\). This instantiates Girard's theorem: \[\text{Excess} = (\alpha+\beta+\gamma)-\pi = \frac{\text{Area}}{R^2}.\] Angle excess per unit area = \(1/R^2\): an intrinsic meter of curvature, measurable by surveying alone with no view from outside. Hyperbolic geometry is the mirror case: deficit instead of excess, \(1/R^2\) negative.

Beltrami (1868) proved hyperbolic geometry is as consistent as Euclidean geometry — modeled faithfully inside it. The parallel postulate is independent: a choice, to be settled (if at all) by physics.

Ch. 15

1827
Gauss

Gauss: the Intrinsic Geometry of Surfaces

Gauss (1827) asked: which geometric properties of a surface are intrinsic — available to an ant that cannot look at the embedding space? A surface carries a metric \(ds^2 = E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2\) — a position-dependent Pythagoras. Everything intrinsic flows from it: lengths, angles, areas, and geodesics.

Theorema Egregium: the curvature \(K\) is expressible entirely in terms of \(E,F,G\) and their derivatives. Curvature is intrinsic.

The ant can measure curvature. Three intrinsic fingerprints of \(K\): (1) a geodesic circle of small radius \(r\) has circumference \(C(r) = 2\pi r(1-\tfrac{K}{6}r^2+\cdots)\) — measure \(C\) and \(r\), extract \(K\) from the shortfall; (2) triangle angle excess \(= \int K\,dA\); (3) \(K\equiv 0\) if and only if the surface can be mapped flat without distortion — which is why no flat chart of the Earth can preserve all distances. Without the Theorema Egregium, general relativity would be metaphysics.

Ch. 16

10 June 1854
Riemann's lecture

Riemann: Manifolds, Metric, Curvature

For his Göttingen habilitation — the topic chosen by Gauss, the one Riemann least expected — Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) re-founded geometry in one hour with almost no formulas.

  • Manifolds: the primitive notion is an \(n\)-dimensional continuum of points labeled by \(n\) coordinates — with no geometry presupposed.
  • Metric: geometry enters as an additional structure, a rule for infinitesimal length: \(ds^2 = \sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(x)\,dx^i dx^j\), generalizing Gauss's surface form to \(n\) dimensions.
  • Curvature: to each point and each 2-plane direction through it, a number (the sectional curvature) generalizing Gauss's \(K\).
  • The empirical coda: "the ground of the metric relations must be sought outside [the manifold], in binding forces acting upon it." Geometry, Riemann suggests, may be physics. He tried himself; the tools and the physics of his day were not ready. Sixty-one years later, Einstein completed the thought.

Curvature as holonomy. Transport a vector around a closed loop; in flat space it returns unchanged. On a sphere, transport along the triangle of Ch. 14 (equator → 90° of longitude → back over the pole): the vector returns rotated by 90° — exactly the triangle's angle excess, i.e. \(\int K\,dA\) over the enclosed region. The rotation-per-loop-area is the curvature, and no external view was used. This "failure to come back the same" is what the Riemann tensor measures, and its physical name is tidal field.

Ch. 17

1869–1915
Tensor calculus

Tensor Calculus: Christoffel, Ricci, Levi-Civita

On a curved manifold there are no preferred coordinates. The ordinary derivative of a vector field is not coordinate-independent: in polar coordinates, a constant vector field has nonzero component-derivatives because the basis vectors themselves turn. Needed: a derivative that subtracts the basis's own variation.

Where the Christoffel symbols come from. Demand two properties of parallel transport: (i) it preserves lengths and angles — the metric is covariantly constant, \(\nabla_k g_{ij}=0\); (ii) no torsion: \(\Gamma^k_{ij}=\Gamma^k_{ji}\). Write condition (i) three times with indices cycled, add two, subtract the third: the symmetry makes terms cancel pairwise, isolating: \[\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{lj}+\partial_j g_{li}-\partial_l g_{ij}).\] These are the unique connection determined by the metric. Physically: once you know how to measure distances, you know how free particles move — one structure, not two. This is why, in GR, the metric alone is the gravitational field.

Curvature = commutator of covariant derivatives. \[[\nabla_i,\nabla_j]V^k = R^k{}_{lij}V^l, \quad R^k{}_{lij} = \partial_i\Gamma^k_{jl}-\partial_j\Gamma^k_{il}+\Gamma^k_{im}\Gamma^m_{jl}-\Gamma^k_{jm}\Gamma^m_{il}.\] Flat \(\Leftrightarrow R^k{}_{lij}=0\). The Bianchi identities \(\nabla_{[m}R^k{}_{|l|ij]}=0\) imply \[\nabla^\mu\!\left(R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu}\right)=0,\] which is why exactly this combination — the Einstein tensor — can equal the conserved \(T_{\mu\nu}\).

Geodesic deviation = tidal force. Two nearby free-fallers accelerate relative to each other at a rate given by the Riemann tensor: \[\frac{D^2\xi^\mu}{D\tau^2} = -R^\mu{}_{\nu\alpha\beta}\,u^\nu\xi^\alpha u^\beta.\] Matching to Newton's tidal tensor \(\ddot\xi^i = -\partial^i\partial_j\phi\,\xi^j\) identifies \(R^i{}_{0j0} \leftrightarrow \partial^i\partial_j\phi/c^2\): Riemann curvature is the relativistic tidal field.

Ricci and Levi-Civita published the summa of this "absolute differential calculus" in Mathematische Annalen (1900) — met with indifference. It was exactly the instrument Einstein needed: in 1912 Grossmann put the 1900 memoir in Einstein's hands. The 1915 field equations are written in Ricci's calculus, about Riemann's geometry, selected by Bianchi's identities, solved along Christoffel's geodesics.
